Companion to Life of a Frame. That guide answers what the code does, in the order a frame does it. This one answers why it exists and how it got that way, in the order the decisions were made. Neither repeats the other: where the mechanism matters, this guide links across rather than re-explaining it; a dashed link with an arrow opens the other guide.

Reader's guide

Quake's source reads like a set of answers with the questions filed off. The expanded collision hulls, the precomputed visibility, the surface cache, the 20 Hz server tick — each is a settled solution to a problem that was live and unsolved in 1995, and several are the survivors of approaches that were built, measured, and thrown away. This guide reconstructs those questions from the public record: Michael Abrash's contemporaneous chapters, the developers' .plan files, and id's own announcements, all archived under docs/historical/.

The material here is evidence-bounded. A claim about what the code does is checkable against the code and is marked with a source reference. A claim about what someone was thinking is only as good as the document it came from, so those are quoted and attributed rather than paraphrased into confident narrative. Where the record is thin, the guide says so instead of filling the gap.

Dates are as published. .plan files were daily worklogs written for a public finger query, not design documents — they are terse, occasionally wrong, and record what was done that day rather than why. Abrash's chapters were written during and shortly after the work, and are the closest thing to a design record that exists.

The shape of the story

Jan 1993
DOOM announced. A feature list that defines the ceiling Quake would have to break: texture-mapped with variable-height floors and ceilings, but no true 3-D geometry and sprites for moving things.
Mar 1994
Quake announced as a design, with no engine and development deferred until fall 1994. Romero's .plan promises a step over DOOM as large as DOOM was over Wolfenstein.
Mar 1995
Abrash joins id. A prototype engine and a plan already exist. Within three months, almost none of the original visibility design survives.
1995
The year of discarded renderers. Beam trees, portal clipping, direct-BSP visibility — built, measured, abandoned. Then the PVS.
1996
Making it fit. Surface caching, Pentium-specific assembly, dialup as an architectural input. Quake ships in June.
Dec 1996
VQuake. The first accelerated Quake, on hardware programmable enough to keep the software renderer's architecture intact.
1997
After ship. GLQuake concedes the software rasterizer's future; QuakeWorld revisits the networking decision Quake declined to make; per-vendor ports stop being affordable.
