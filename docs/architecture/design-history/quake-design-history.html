Reader's guide

Quake's source reads like a set of answers with the questions filed off. The expanded collision hulls, the precomputed visibility, the surface cache, the 20 Hz server tick — each is a settled solution to a problem that was live and unsolved in 1995, and several are the survivors of approaches that were built, measured, and thrown away. This guide reconstructs those questions from the public record: Michael Abrash's contemporaneous chapters, the developers' .plan files, and id's own announcements, all archived under docs/historical/.

The material here is evidence-bounded. A claim about what the code does is checkable against the code and is marked with a source reference. A claim about what someone was thinking is only as good as the document it came from, so those are quoted and attributed rather than paraphrased into confident narrative. Where the record is thin, the guide says so instead of filling the gap.

Selected claims carry evidence tags: Code checked against source code; Source reported by a cited document, including paraphrases; Inference reconstructed from the evidence. Tags do not replace citations.

Dates are as published. .plan files were daily worklogs written for a public finger query, not design documents — they are terse, occasionally wrong, and record what was done that day rather than why. Abrash's chapters were written during and shortly after the work, and are the closest thing to a design record that exists.

01 · OriginsBefore Quake: what DOOM could not do

Quake's architecture is best understood as a series of answers to the limitations of DOOM's engine. Released in December 1993, DOOM used a two-dimensional binary space partitioning (BSP) tree, sprites for moving objects, and a fixed camera orientation. While these design choices enabled playable frame rates on 386 computers, they became constraints that Quake's engine had to overcome. This chapter establishes the nature of these constraints using id Software's own 1993 announcement, John Carmack's later retrospective on the DOOM code, and John Romero's 1994 announcement of Quake, which described the game before its engine existed.

First pass. DOOM's BSP tree was two-dimensional and used only for drawing. In contrast, Quake's BSP is three-dimensional and used for rendering, collision detection, and network visibility. Nearly all of Quake's runtime characteristics stem from the expansion of this single data structure and the subsequent effort to leverage it fully. Next: the year of prototypes that determined how to use it.

#The 1993 feature list as a ceiling

id's DOOM press release from January 1, 1993 is unusually detailed about the engine's capabilities (doom_press_release_1993.txt). It promotes a "fantastic fully texture-mapped environment," non-orthogonal walls, light diminishing and sourcing, variable-height floors and ceilings, environment animation, palette translation, and four-player networked play. Carmack provides a performance target: "on a 486/33, we're talking 35 frames per second, fully texture-mapped at normal detail, for a large area of the screen."

This list merits a second reading, as its omissions are the true focus of this guide. Notably absent are any mentions of vertical camera movement, rooms stacked on top of rooms, geometry at angles to the floor, or three-dimensional creatures. These are not mere oversights in the marketing copy; they lie outside the representational capabilities of the engine. DOOM's world is essentially a floor plan with heights attached. Walls are vertical, floors and ceilings horizontal, and no two sectors can occupy the same footprint at different elevations.

However, two core commitments from this list carried forward into Quake, and they warrant mention now as later chapters rely on them. The first is light diminishing and light sourcing—the recognition that lighting is crucial for environmental immersion and therefore deserves a substantial portion of the frame budget. Quake's approach is significantly more ambitious and is discussed in the surface caching chapter, but the underlying commitment is evident here. The second is the section titled "An Open Game":

When our last hit, WOLFENSTEIN 3D was released the public responded with an almost immediate deluge of home-brewed utilities; map editors, sound editors, trainers, etc. All without any help on file formats or game layout from Id Software. DOOM will be release as an OPEN GAME. We will provide file formats and technical notes for anyone who wants them. — id Software, DOOM press release, 1 January 1993

This 1993 statement articulates an architectural policy that Quake takes much further, from published file formats to a shipped bytecode virtual machine with the game logic compiled into a replaceable progs.dat. The QuakeC VM can be seen as a descendant of this policy. Inference The QuakeC chapter explores this lineage.

#A binary tree in two dimensions

DOOM's central data structure was a BSP tree, and Carmack's use of it popularized the technique. Michael Abrash, writing in 1995 while working with Carmack, explicitly notes the dimensionality: while searching for Quake's visibility algorithm, Carmack "spent a week on this, as a byproduct devising a perfect DOOM (2-D) visibility architecture, whereby a single, linear walk of a DOOM BSP tree produces zero-overdraw 2-D visibility" (Black Book, ch. 64, "Quake's Visible-Surface Determination"). The parenthetical clarification carries weight. A DOOM BSP divides the map with lines on a plan view; every splitter is a wall segment, and the tree partitions a floor plan.

This directly constrains the geometry: walls are the map's linedefs, while floors and ceilings are sector properties the renderer fills in between them. In his notes accompanying the DOOM source release on December 23, 1997 (carmack_doom_retrospective.txt), Carmack identified precisely this structure as the element he would change:

The way the rendering proceded from walls to floors to sprites could be collapsed into a single front-to-back walk of the bsp tree to collect information, then draw all the contents of a subsector on the way back up the tree. It requires treating floors and ceilings as polygons, rather than just the gaps between walls, and it requires clipping sprite billboards into subsector fragments, but it would be The Right Thing. — John Carmack, DOOM source release notes, 23 December 1997

Quake eliminates this constraint at the root of the representation. Its splitting planes carry a full normal vector, not an implied vertical: Code

The critical difference is that normal is a vec3_t. Polygons can be oriented at any angle, enabling ramps and arbitrary architecture; leaves are convex volumes rather than convex floor regions, allowing rooms to stack; and the tree can resolve queries about points anywhere in 3D space, not just points on a plan. The tradeoff is a significantly more complex compile process. Abrash's chapter 60, "Compiling BSP Trees" constructs one in two dimensions, over line segments, to illustrate the technique; building one over arbitrary 3D polygons is the responsibility of qbsp, and the splitter heuristic that made it feasible is documented in chapter 64. The more challenging consequence is visibility, which the year of discarded renderers covers. The on-disk BSP format and the node and leaf graph it loads into are detailed in Life of a Frame.

#The BSP used only for drawing

The dimensional change is the obvious difference. The more impactful one is what the tree is used for, and here the best source is Carmack's assessment of his own code four years later:

The movement and line of sight checking against the lines is one of the bigger misses that I look back on. It is messy code that had some failure cases, and there was a vastly simpler (and faster) solution sitting in front of my face. I used the BSP tree for rendering things, but I didn't realize at the time that it could also be used for environment testing. — John Carmack, DOOM source release notes, 23 December 1997

In DOOM, the BSP tree was constructed for the renderer. Movement and hitscan traversed the linedefs via the blockmap; line of sight walked the BSP. Quake unifies rendering and environment testing around the same compiled map geometry: rendering traverses the render BSP, while collision traverses one of the pre-expanded clip hulls, reusing the map's planes. Life of a Frame covers the mechanism in detail: SV_RecursiveHullCheck is the descent, and contents types explain how the engine separates render nodes and collision hulls.

Carmack's retrospective proceeds directly to the cost of this approach:

Sweeping volumes for movement gets a bit tougher, and touches on many of the challenges faced in quake / quake2 with edge bevels on polyhedrons. — John Carmack, DOOM source release notes, 23 December 1997

This is the genesis of one of Quake's most distinctive and least intuitive structures. Sweeping a player-sized box through a 3D tree is genuinely challenging; reducing that box to a point moving through a tree whose solid volumes have been pre-expanded by the box's dimensions is straightforward and reuses the descent unchanged. Consequently, qbsp compiles not one tree but several, and each model carries all of them:

with MAX_MAP_HULLS set to 4 in WinQuake/bspfile.h:24. The world model uses hulls 0, 1, and 2; slot 3 is unused by the original runtime. SV_HullForEntity selects between them based solely on width (WinQuake/world.c:147-153): under 3 units gets the point hull, up to 32 gets hull 1, and anything larger gets hull 2. As a result, players and most monsters share hull 1, which is 32x32x56; hull 2, at 64x64x88, is for large monsters (WinQuake/model.c:959-981).

This explains why body sizes come in discrete classes rather than being arbitrary per-entity dimensions: collision volumes are compiled into the map, not computed at runtime. This constraint also governed crouching. Hull 1's vertical extent is standing height, and reducing an entity's bounding box at runtime does not alter the map hull traced against; crouching would have required its own compiled hull. Slot 3 was reserved in the format for a fourth hull, but id never populated it, and the archive does not document why. The three hulls and how an entity selects or fabricates one are covered in Life of a Frame. The "edge bevels" Carmack mentions are the correction needed to maintain convex expanded polyhedra—a detail that only becomes an issue once you commit to this strategy.

The third use of the tree has no direct analog in DOOM. Because a leaf is a volume, and because visibility between volumes can be computed offline, the same spatial structure that answers "what do I draw" can answer "what does this client need to be told about." Quake's server gates entity updates on the potentially visible set of the leaf each client occupies. This is a networking decision built on a rendering data structure, and the shipping-constraints chapter investigates how dialup bandwidth influenced it.

#Announced as a design, not an engine

Quake was public long before it was real. John Romero's .plan from March 18, 1994 announces it by name, with a subtitle, a comparison, and a start date (john_romero/1994-03-18.txt):

.plan. The next game is going to blow DOOM all to hell. DOOM totally sucks in comparison to our next game, QUAKE: The Fight for Justice! QUAKE is going to be a bigger step over DOOM than DOOM was over Wolf3D (ya know -- DOOM = pong). We won't start developing QUAKE until Fall'94, due to Jaguar DOOM (Comlynxed!), commercial DOOM (COMPUSA, Babbage's, etc.) and maybe a DOOM level pack. — John Romero, .plan, 18 March 1994

Two aspects stand out. The first is the ambition, expressed as a ratio rather than a feature: the leap from DOOM to Quake was intended to be at least as significant as the leap from Wolfenstein to DOOM. Wolfenstein's walls were axis-aligned and eight feet thick; DOOM's could be at any angle and any thickness. The announcement does not specify what the next leap would entail; the shipped engine made it a full third dimension. Inference The chapters on discarded renderers and surface caching, followed by the one-processor chapter, describe the cost.

The second is the timeline. Quake was announced in March 1994, development was not scheduled to begin until autumn, and the game shipped in June 1996. Abrash did not join id until March 1995 (Black Book, ch. 64), by which point "John already had an engine prototyped and a plan in mind" (Abrash, ch. 64)—a plan that the year of discarded renderers examines and that largely failed to survive contact with the problem. The subtitle The Fight for Justice did not survive either. What shipped shares an ambition with the announcement and little else, a useful calibration of how much of Quake's design was intended in advance and how much was discovered under deadline.

The same .plan incidentally records the development environment—"To get the entire world running NEXTSTEP for development" (Romero, .plan, 18 March 1994). NeXTSTEP explains the presence of a NeXT build path in the source and the manner in which the tools are written.

02 · VisibilityThe year of discarded renderers

Throughout 1995 and the first half of 1996, Quake's visible-surface determination algorithm underwent a series of complete reconstructions. A beam tree was implemented, optimized, and ultimately discarded. At least five other approaches were prototyped or seriously considered before being abandoned. The final design, which precomputes the set of potentially visible leaves for each leaf in the map and then draws only those leaves at runtime, was not part of the original plan. It emerged a few months after Michael Abrash joined the team, roughly a year before release. This chapter traces that evolution, as understanding the surviving code requires knowing its history: the potentially visible set might seem like an obvious solution in hindsight, but viewing it that way misses the true cost of the approach.

First pass. Quake's challenge was never "how to draw polygons quickly." Rather, it was overdraw: in a level with ten thousand polygons, most of what falls inside the view frustum is occluded by other geometry, and drawing it anyway wastes work that scales with level complexity. The discarded approaches attempted to make world visibility manageable at runtime. The shipped design moved that work offline, with runtime rendering still resolving pixel visibility. Next: surface caching and the cost of lighting.

#Defining the problem precisely

In chapter 64, "Quake's Visible-Surface Determination," Abrash lays out the ideal target, and keeping this definition in mind is crucial because every rejected approach failed to meet one of its requirements:

The process of VSD would ideally work as follows: First, you would cull all polygons that are completely outside the view frustum (view pyramid), and would clip away the irrelevant portions of any polygons that are partially outside. Then, you would draw only those pixels of each polygon that are actually visible from the current viewpoint [...] Finally, in a perfect world, the tests to figure out what parts of which polygons are visible would be free, and the processing time would be the same for all possible viewpoints, giving the game a smooth visual flow. — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 64

Two aspects were straightforward. A BSP allows efficient coarse frustum culling, as each node bounds a convex subspace, so rejecting a node rejects all polygons beneath it. It also provides a front-to-back or back-to-front traversal order. The difficult parts were the middle clause and, most crucially, the last one.

The final clause is where the trouble lies. A painter's algorithm renderer draws every polygon inside the frustum, hidden or not; Abrash estimates that in a typical Quake level, worst-case overdraw could be "10 times or more" (Abrash, ch. 64), meaning each pixel is written an average of ten times. The issue is not just the slowdown. It's that best-case and worst-case performance diverge significantly, causing frame rate to fluctuate as the player's view changes. A design that runs smoothly in corridors but crawls to a halt in large spaces has not truly solved the problem.

#The prototype's lifespan

When Abrash arrived at id in March 1995, he expected to optimize an existing design:

When I arrived at id at the beginning of March 1995, John already had an engine prototyped and a plan in mind, and I assumed that our work was a simple matter of finishing and optimizing that engine. If I had been aware of id's history, however, I would have known better. — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 64

The correction is instructive. By Abrash's count, Carmack had by then written "perhaps 20 distinct engines over a four-year period," including "four engines in four weeks" on one occasion. Rewriting the renderer was the project's normal state, not a crisis. Three months after Abrash joined, "only one element of the original VSD design was anywhere in sight" (Abrash, ch. 64).

This context is essential for understanding Quake's source code. The code is not a first draft that was optimized; it is the last survivor of a lengthy process of elimination.

#The beam tree

The original design rendered front-to-back and maintained a second BSP tree, a beam tree, to track which parts of the screen were still unpainted. This tree was three-dimensional: a set of wedges bounded by planes radiating from the viewpoint.

It begins as a single beam — the view frustum — with everything outside marked solid and the inside empty. Traversing the world tree front-to-back, each polygon is converted into a beam by running planes from its edges through the viewpoint. Any part of that beam intersecting empty space in the beam tree is visible and is added to the beam tree as solid. When the beam tree becomes entirely solid, nothing further can be visible, and the traversal stops. Drawing occurs afterwards: once the tree is complete, the visible portions of the polygons that contributed to it are rasterized.

The reason for using 3-D beams instead of 2-D screen regions is arithmetic efficiency. Since every beam plane passes through the viewpoint, testing a vertex against one is just a dot product with the plane normal and a sign check, and generating a beam from a polygon edge is a single cross product. There is no projection or division in the inner test (Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 64). Source

The advantage to working with a 3-D beam tree, rather than a 2-D region, is that determining which side of a beam plane a polygon vertex is on involves only checking the sign of the dot product of the ray to the vertex and the plane normal, because all beam planes run through the origin (the viewpoint). — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 64, "The Beam Tree".

The beam tree failed to meet the last requirement of the specification. While early termination appeared to cap the worst case, a view across the top of a level or out to the sky never filled the beam tree, so every polygon inside the frustum was tested anyway. Numerical precision also left thin cracks that prevented the tree from closing completely. Abrash concludes:

in scenes with long view distances, such as views across the top of a level, the total cost of beam processing slowed Quake's frame rate to a crawl. So, in the end, the beam-tree approach proved to suffer from much the same malady as the painter's algorithm: The worst case was much worse than the average case, and it didn't scale well with increasing level complexity. — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 64

#Five approaches that did not ship

Abrash lists the alternatives that were prototyped "in overnight or weekend-long bursts of coding" (Abrash, ch. 64) or considered and rejected. They are worth noting individually, as each one fails differently, mapping out the design space.

Subdividing raycast. Cast rays on an 8×8 screen grid — inexpensive, as a ray is just a clip into the BSP tree until it reaches a solid leaf. Where adjacent rays hit different surfaces, cast another ray between them, recursively, until neighbors agree or are adjacent pixels. This scales with pixel count rather than polygon count and eliminates overdraw entirely: exactly the right scaling behavior. It was rejected for correctness, not speed — small polygons can fall between rays and disappear.

Vertex-free surfaces. Store the world as planes only, extracting polygons from plane intersections just before drawing. Clipping becomes fast, and the dataset shrinks considerably, as a plane is much more compact than a polygon. Rejected because extracting polygons from planes is expensive at precisely the wrong moment. The shipped on-disk BSP does pool and share planes, but that is standard BSP optimization rather than a remnant of this idea: the vertex-free proposal was to not store polygons at all.

The draw-buffer. An occlusion bitmap with one bit per pixel, recording only whether that pixel has been written. This eliminates overdraw at the cost of a test in the inner loop, extra writes, cache misses, and, in Abrash's summary, "considerable complexity" (Abrash, ch. 64). The variations considered illustrate how far the search went: test the buffer a byte at a time and skip fully occluded bytes, or branch from each byte into one of 256 unrolled loops for drawing 0–8 pixels, hoping to overlap the perspective divide with pixel processing.

Span-based drawing. Rasterize polygons into spans, clipping each new span against a global span list, keeping only the nearest at each pixel. Front-to-back traversal means little sorting is needed — if there is overlap, the span already in the list is nearer. No overdraw, but every polygon still has to be turned into spans, and the span arithmetic is substantial. This is the closest of the five to what shipped. The edge list and span sorting in the final renderer belong to the same family, though Abrash arrives at edge sorting separately in chapters 66 and 67 rather than by reviving this proposal. The section below returns to it.

Portals. Track the holes in surfaces, as line-of-sight only extends through them. Draw front-to-back; on reaching a portal, clip the polygons and portals behind it to its limits, recursively, until nothing visible remains. This draws only the visible parts of visible polygons — the ideal — but pays for it with extensive portal clipping.

The portal approach is the one to keep in mind: the shipped PVS preserves its connectivity insight but precomputes visibility per leaf instead of clipping portals at runtime.

#The breakthrough

Carmack next tried extracting visibility directly from the world BSP tree, reasoning that the beam tree was a second-order structure holding information the world tree already contained implicitly. A week of that produced, as a side effect, a perfect zero-overdraw visibility algorithm for two-dimensional DOOM trees — and, in three dimensions, mounting complexity and persistent glitches. Abrash's account of the weekend that followed is the chapter's most-quoted passage:

When I came in on Monday, John had the look of a man who had broken through to the other side—and also the look of a man who hadn't had much sleep. [...] At 3:30 Monday morning, as he lay in bed, thinking about portals, he thought of precalculating and storing in each leaf a list of all leaves visible from that leaf, and then at runtime just drawing the visible leaves back-to-front for whatever leaf the viewpoint happens to be in, ignoring all other leaves entirely. — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 64

The shift was to stop computing world visibility at runtime and start looking it up. The discarded approaches tried to make that calculation cheaper; the PVS removes it from the frame loop entirely. Abrash draws the general lesson through a colleague's aphorism — "almost all programming can be viewed as an exercise in caching" (Abrash, ch. 64) — and credits the reframing as the inspired part, rather than the algorithm itself.

The archive does not provide an exact date for this, but it can be bounded: Abrash joined in March 1995; chapter 64, written "several months" into his time there with "a few more months to go" (Abrash, ch. 64), describes the PVS prototype as already working and was published while Quake was still unshipped; the game shipped in June 1996. That places the breakthrough in 1995, most likely around the middle of the year and thus roughly a year before release. The archive supports no tighter estimate than that. Inference

Abrash records a key correction: Carmack did not experience it as a eureka moment. Abrash reports him saying "precalculating the PVS was a logical evolution of the approaches he had been considering, that there was no moment when he said 'Eureka!'" (Abrash, ch. 64). The dramatic version is Abrash's framing of a colleague's work; the participant's version is that it was a logical next step. Note also what the preceding week was actually spent on: extracting visibility directly from the world BSP tree, not portals. Portals enter the picture at 3:30 Monday morning, as the subject Carmack was pondering when the precomputation idea emerged. Abrash reports both accounts within a few paragraphs of each other, and the gap between them reveals something about how such designs come about.

Nor was the underlying concept unprecedented. Abrash's own reference list for the chapter cites Seth Teller's Visibility Preprocessing for Interactive Walkthroughs (SIGGRAPH 91) and his dissertation on visibility in densely occluded polyhedral environments. The archive does not record whether that work was an input; it does establish that precomputed visibility was known in the literature, and that the achievement was making it fit in a 1996 consumer product.

#Making it fit

Abrash reports that the first raw, uncompressed PVS was several megabytes. Four changes brought it down to roughly 20 KB for a good-sized level (Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 64): Source

Size was a concern; initially, a raw, uncompressed potentially visible set (PVS) was several megabytes in size. […] Those steps, along with changing the BSP heuristic to generate fewer leaves (choosing as the next splitter the polygon that splits the fewest other polygons appears to be the best heuristic) and sealing the outside of the levels so the BSPer can remove the outside surfaces, which can never be seen, eventually brought the PVS down to about 20 Kb for a good-size level. — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 64, "Breakthrough!".

  1. Store the set as a bit vector, one bit per leaf, rather than a list.
  2. Compress it with run-length encoding of zero bytes, which works extremely well because the rows are sparse. Life of a Frame covers the decompressor.
  3. Change the BSP splitter heuristic to produce fewer leaves. Abrash: "choosing as the next splitter the polygon that splits the fewest other polygons appears to be the best heuristic" (Abrash, ch. 64).
  4. Seal the outside of levels, so the compiler can discard exterior surfaces that can never be seen.

The fourth point explains a rule every Quake mapper has encountered: a leak prevents the normal sealed-world BSP/PVS build from producing valid visibility data. That constraint is not arbitrary strictness — it is what makes the surface count, and therefore the leaf count, and therefore the size of the visibility data, manageable. The format allows up to a megabyte for it (MAX_MAP_VISIBILITY at WinQuake/bspfile.h:44), while the cited good-sized level used about a fiftieth of that.

#What it bought, and what it cost

The PVS is deliberately imprecise. It records what is visible from anywhere in a leaf, not from the actual viewpoint, so it over-reports. Abrash gives the resulting overdraw as "typically on the order of 50 percent but ranging up to 150 percent" (Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 64). Source

the PVS for a leaf includes all leaves visible from anywhere in the leaf, so some overdraw, typically on the order of 50 percent but ranging up to 150 percent, generally occurs — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 64, "Breakthrough!".

rays from the viewpoint bound a viewing wedge; heavy wall segments mark only the portions visible through gaps between solid blocks. | Figure 64.2 — exact visibility from one viewpoint. That inaccuracy is the whole point. Every rejected approach bought precision with runtime work that grew with scene complexity; the PVS accepts bounded imprecision in exchange for a lookup. The payoff is in the third clause of the original specification:

precalculating the PVS results in a leveling of performance; worst case is no longer much worse than best case, because there's no longer extra VSD processing—just more polygons and perhaps some extra overdraw—associated with complex scenes. The first time John showed me his working prototype, I went to the most complex scene I knew of, a place where the frame rate used to grind down into the single digits, and spun around smoothly, with no perceptible slowdown. — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 64

The cost moved to the map toolchain, where it is paid once by the author rather than on every frame by the player. That trade is why vis is slow enough to have its own folklore. The archive does not quantify vis itself, but it does show the stage becoming a prerequisite for the rest of the toolchain: Carmack's .plan of 27 August 1996, describing his new radiosity lighting tool, notes that "you MUST do a vis before running qrad (otherwise it would take about 1000 times longer)." The thousandfold increase and the tool's 100 MB working set are qrad's, not vis's. The same entry ends with him wanting "to look into" the algorithmic order of full vis processing, "especially now that the vis stage is put before production lighting." Life of a Frame covers how the runtime consumes the result; the GL path keeps a r_novis cvar (WinQuake/gl_rmain.c:86) that switches the whole mechanism off, which is the fastest way to feel what it does.

#The PVS stops being a renderer feature

The clearest evidence that this was a structural decision rather than a rendering optimization is what happened to it afterwards. Carmack's 1996 worklogs show subsystems that have nothing to do with drawing reaching for the PVS. The entries are terse and follow a consistent shape, with * marking completed work and + marking immediate next steps; a plain line is a backlog item. Tracked across six weeks:

.plan. PVS for net update — 15 March 1996, backlog; then * changed network update to client PVS specific — 23 April 1996, done.

That is the moment visibility became a bandwidth policy: the server stopped telling each client about entities the client could not possibly see. The mechanism is SV_FatPVS, and the shipping-constraints chapter examines how dialup shaped it. The same worklogs carry a one-line done-item, "fixed monster sighting PVS problem," on 11 March. That reads like gameplay-visible behavior depending on compiled visibility data, but a single worklog line will not carry the claim on its own, and the archive says nothing further about it. On 6 April Carmack probes for further uses:

.plan. use PVS to speed line of sight checks? Is that any faster than doing the line check, because it involves two point checks? — Carmack, 6 April 1996

Note that this one is a question, and that it is asked on cost grounds rather than accepted on principle. It is a useful corrective to the tidy version of this story: by 1996 the PVS was infrastructure, but its applicability was still being evaluated case by case.

#What the PVS did not solve

The PVS answers "which leaves might be visible." It does not answer "which pixels are visible," and its deliberate over-reporting actively creates overdraw. Something still has to resolve the remainder.

That is where span-based drawing, rejected on its own above, returns. Abrash describes the shipped renderer at the end of the chapter as the PVS "together with a still-in-development sorted-edge rasterizer that completely eliminates overdraw" (Abrash, ch. 64). The two halves are complementary: the PVS discards most of the world for free, and the edge list resolves what is left exactly. Neither would have been sufficient alone, and the PVS's tolerance for imprecision is affordable precisely because a zero-overdraw rasterizer sits behind it.

Quake still uses a depth buffer for entities, particles and sprites. The world writes depth as it is rasterized, so dynamic geometry can be tested per pixel without making the world depend on a general z-buffer. That is a separate decision, reached in chapter 66, not the rejected draw-buffer coming back: the draw-buffer was a one-bit occlusion mask for the world, and this is per-pixel depth for everything drawn after it. What they share is the recognition that an occlusion structure is affordable once it covers only the geometry that needs one.

The implementation details belong in Life of a Frame. The design decision is the cost split: the PVS discards most of the world cheaply, while the edge list and depth buffer resolve what remains at runtime.

03 · LightingSurface caching: making light affordable

Chapter 2 got Quake down to drawing only the pixels that are actually visible. Lighting posed the opposite kind of problem: how to keep shadows and highlights attached to the world without paying for them in polygons.

Michael Abrash's chapter 68, "Quake's Lighting Model" traces the route from vertex lighting to lightmaps to pre-lit cached surfaces. The idea was to separate a wall's fine texture detail from its coarser illumination, then reuse the combined result. Chapter 69 asks how well that design holds up when a graphics card takes over the drawing.

First pass. Moving world lighting off polygon vertices and onto a coarse lightmap, combined with the texture into a cached pre-lit surface, bought consistent lighting and a simpler pixel loop. It cost memory, and it made every change in illumination a rebuild. Next: the processor those loops were written for.

#The problem with vertex lighting

Abrash opens chapter 68 with the goal for the world renderer: geometry, texture and lighting all stay fixed and consistent as the viewpoint moves. That is a higher bar than moving models had to clear, and they got their own faster code paths. Everything below is about static world geometry — walls, floors, ceilings.

Quake started with Gouraud shading: a lighting value per polygon vertex, interpolated across the face. That made the geometry do two jobs — describe the wall's shape, and supply enough sample points for its illumination. A large flat wall needs a handful of polygons for its shape; a spotlight on it, or a shadow across it, needs many more vertices to be represented at all.

Carmack's first fix was to add a vertex under each spotlight and split the surrounding polygon. Abrash reports better highlights — and the problem laid bare: more polygons to store, transform and rasterize, none of them adding geometric detail. Shadows and overlapping lights would each demand their own subdivision. The defect was the coupling itself. Source

A primary problem with Gouraud shading is that it requires the vertices used for world geometry to serve as lighting sample points as well, even though there isn't necessarily a close relationship between lighting and geometry. This artificial coupling often forces the subdivision of a single polygon into several polygons purely for lighting reasons — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 68, "Problems with Gouraud Shading".

#The limits of finer tessellation

The flaw showed itself on a lit floor viewed from different angles: because the lighting interpolation was not perspective-correct, illumination slid across the texture as the polygon's screen orientation changed. Rasterizing in triangles removed that rotational ambiguity, but left lighting tied to clipping and projection.

Finer tessellation could mask the artifacts, at the price of everything chapter 2 had spent on getting the polygon count down: a bigger world database and more rasterization. It suits dynamic lighting worst of all, because a highlight that moves needs a sample point where the compiler did not put one.

Abrash describes weeks of considering alternatives while the Gouraud code stayed in. The turn came when Carmack looked at a wall split for a spotlight, with a lighting discontinuity along the resulting T-junction, and wanted the wall back as one surface. The chapter gives no date.

#Decoupling lighting from geometry

The answer was to store lighting samples in a separate grid per polygon — a lightmap — with no relation to the vertices. It is computed offline, one sample every 16 texels horizontally and vertically. At runtime the engine combines that coarse lighting with the tiled texture into a pre-lit surface, then draws the surface with perspective-correct texture mapping and no lighting in the pixel loop at all. Source

During off-line preprocessing, a grid, called a light map, is calculated for each polygon in the world, with a lighting value every 16 texels horizontally and vertically. […] Then the polygon is drawn with perspective texture mapping, with the surface serving as the input texture, and with no lighting performed during the texture mapping. — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 68, "Decoupling Lighting from Rasterization".

Lighting is now sampled and filtered on its own grid, independently of the texture detail. A sample every 16 texels cannot capture every fine shadow, but it lights the wall without adding polygons.

Because lighting and texture are combined before perspective projection, they stay in registration through every transform and clip. The consistency problem is solved in the representation rather than patched in the rasterizer. Life of a Frame covers the lightmap format and the runtime surface build.

#Caching combined surfaces

The lightmap is only half the design. The other half is caching the combined surfaces, on the expectation that most of what is visible this frame was visible last frame. If a surface is going to be reused, keeping its lit texels avoids rebuilding them.

Abrash reports that the cache was part of the idea from the start, because Carmack expected compositing to be expensive. Source

When he thought of surface-based lighting, John immediately realized that surface building would be relatively expensive. […] Consequently, his design included the concept of caching surfaces, so that if the same surface were visible in the next frame, it could be reused without having to be rebuilt. — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 68, "Surface Caching".

Splitting surface build from surface draw also left two short loops instead of one long one, each easy to optimize. Abrash gives 2.25 cycles per lit texel for the builder and 7.5 cycles per pixel for the perspective mapper on a Pentium. These are inner-loop timings in isolation, not per-frame costs, and texels built are not pixels drawn: a cache hit builds nothing, while a partially occluded surface builds texels that are never seen.

D_CacheSurface reuses a surface only when the requested mip level has cached pixels with the same texture animation frame and all four light-style values unchanged. No dynamic light may affect the surface this frame, and the cached pixels must contain no previous dynamic lighting (WinQuake/d_surf.c:264-288). Code A surface must therefore be rebuilt when a dynamic light disappears, too. The runtime caching section covers reuse and allocation in detail.

#Balancing quality and memory

Combining texture and lighting requires separate storage for each cached surface. Surfaces can share a 64x64 source texture, but different lighting produces different sets of lit texels. Abrash's first estimate was several megabytes even at 320x200 — too much for the 8 MB target machine — and the composited texels have far worse CPU cache behaviour than a 64x64 tile referenced over and over.

Two existing mechanisms kept the cache small. The edge list prevents fully hidden surfaces from being built. Mipmapping quarters the texel count at each level, so distant surfaces need less storage.

Both have limits. A partially visible surface is still built in full. Mip level is chosen per surface, from the distance of its nearest vertex, so a floor spanning a large depth range is built at the near level throughout. That is what the 256x256 cap on surface dimensions is for: without it, floors would run to thousands of texels at their nearest vertex's resolution. Lighting quality is no longer a function of polygon density, but surface size is still capped to keep the cache affordable.

Chapter 68 reports that 600 KB proved to be plenty for the cache at 320x200, and a little over 1 MB at 640x480. Carmack kept adjusting the cache budget as release approached. Carmack's 24 May 1996 worklog carries two unstarred backlog lines — "need bigger surface cache in higher res modes" and "check surface cache size performance figures" — so the resolution-dependent sizing was a known open item three weeks before ship, not yet a measurement. It closes on 12 June:

.plan. * increased surface cache size — Carmack, 12 June 1996.

The log records neither the new size nor any performance figure.

#Limits of the caching approach

Combining lighting into the cached texels means any change in illumination invalidates them. Abrash's worst case is a frame in which every visible surface must be rebuilt: no reuse at all, and the full cost of building and drawing. He calls the slowdown tolerable but noticeable, and advises capping how many surfaces may be invalidated at once.

That is a constraint on level design, not just on code: static architectural lighting plays to the cache, and dynamic or animated lighting plays against it. The trade-off follows from the documented reuse behaviour. The archive does not connect it to any level designer's intent. Inference

Chapter 68 closes by floating a further use for the cache: compositing bullet holes, decals and other persistent marks into the surface. Shipped Quake does not do this. The idea is what the architecture allows, not what was built.

#What hardware changed

Chapter 69 revisits the trade-off from Abrash's work porting Quake to Rendition's Vérité. The architecture survives the move: the CPU still builds surfaces, but now downloads them into the card's memory, and they must be 16 bpp for the hardware's bilinear filter. That doubles the data and roughly doubles the build cost, so a cache miss costs well over twice what it costs in software — and a higher-resolution framebuffer both enlarges the working set and shrinks the memory left to hold it. Source

The surface-caching architecture of the Verite version of Quake (which we call VQuake) is essentially the same as in the software-only version of Quake: The CPU builds surfaces on demand, which are then downloaded to the accelerator's memory and cached there. […] it takes about twice as long to build 16-bpp surfaces as 8-bpp surfaces, so the cost of missing the surface cache is well over twice as expensive in VQuake as in Quake. — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 69, "Surface Caching with Hardware Assistance".

The alternative Abrash sets against it is two-pass drawing: the unlit texture first, then the lightmap over it as an alpha texture. That keeps the decoupling and the perspective-correct registration with no composite storage and no dependence on a hit rate, and pays for it in fill rate — twice the rasterization. Which of the two wins is a property of the hardware, not of the technique.

The architecture is two separable techniques. Moving lighting into lightmaps is the representational change, and it is what buys the quality and the perspective consistency. Caching the composite on top of it is an optimization chosen for the cost structure of a software rasterizer. Hardware can overturn the second without touching the first — which is what GLQuake did, and what the VQuake chapter and planned GLQuake chapter take up.

04 · PentiumDesigning for one processor

Surface caching left Quake with a simple texture-mapping pipeline. Making that mapper fast depended on one machine: Intel's Pentium. Quake arranged its work to exploit two properties of that chip — paired integer issue, and integer execution overlapping floating-point division. Abrash's chapter 63, "Floating-Point for Real-Time 3-D" explains why old assumptions about x86 performance no longer held. Quake's software renderer could run on other processors, but its fastest loops were written for the Pentium.

First pass. Rather than just replacing C with faster assembly, Quake's rendering pipeline provided opportunities for the Pentium to execute integer pixel drawing concurrently with perspective correction calculations in the FPU. Carefully crafted assembly unlocked this parallelism, and reduced precision accelerated the divides themselves. Next: shipping constraints, where bandwidth, memory, and deadlines further shaped the engine.

#Two pipes, one instruction stream

The Pentium's U- and V-pipes could execute a pair of integer instructions simultaneously, governed by specific pairing rules. This was a single instruction stream with opportunities for paired issue, not two separate processors. Abrash's chapter 20, "Pentium Rules" details how consecutive instructions could sometimes be issued as a pair. The U-pipe handled a superset of the V-pipe's capabilities. Consequently, fewer instructions did not guarantee fewer cycles.

Instruction dependencies were as critical as instruction selection. Placing two instructions that depend on each other's results in a pair could leave the second pipe stalled. Even valid pairs could stall due to address generation interlocks. Chapter 21, "Unleashing the Pentium's V-Pipe" covers these interlocks and the performance impact of lockstep execution.

These rules mattered to Quake because shorter loops gave the programmer more room to arrange instructions. Chapter 68 describes how separating lighting and tiling from texture mapping let almost all variables stay in registers. Source

moving lighting and tiling into a separate loop from texture mapping allows each of the two loops to be optimized very effectively, with almost all variables kept in registers. — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 68, "Decoupling Lighting from Rasterization".

The link to chapter 3 is structural. By moving lighting out of the pixel loop, surface caching left simpler, more register-friendly code behind. This suggests instruction-level benefits complementing the frame-level ones. However, it doesn't prove that pairing was the original motivation for surface caching, which Abrash attributes to lighting challenges. Inference

#Floating-point stops being the slow alternative

Chapter 63 opens with Abrash reevaluating his prior preference for fixed-point math. Faster floating-point instructions, a pipelined FPU, and the ability to overlap FXCH register stack rotations with other FP work changed the equation. The FPU's eight registers were also separate from the integer set. Floating-point permeated Quake's internals down to the span rasterizer. Source

Quake uses FP all the way down into the inner loop of the span rasterizer, performing several FP operations every 16 pixels. — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 63, "Not Your Father's Floating-Point".

The key factor was no longer an operation's latency alone, but the amount of useful work that could be accomplished before its result was needed. While addition, subtraction and multiplication were pipelined, FDIV occupied the FPU for its entire duration. Integer instructions could still proceed during most of this interval, but a subsequent floating-point operation would halt that progress. Careful scheduling was required to ensure sufficient independent integer work preceded the next FPU instruction.

The two kinds of arithmetic worked together: floating-point calculated perspective coordinates at intervals, while integer math handled the pixels in between. The assembly walkthrough examines the resulting implementation.

#Give the divide something to overlap

A perspective texture mapper cannot simply advance texture coordinates uniformly across an entire receding floor. Quake approximated the curve in short segments. Abrash's chapter 70, "Quake: A Post-Mortem and a Glimpse into the Future" outlines computing perspective-correct coordinates every 16 pixels and linearly interpolating in between. He concedes minor inaccuracies, sometimes visible as ripples, in exchange for bypassing the full calculation every pixel.

Those blocks gave the processor useful work to do while it waited for a divide. After the FPU delivered a span's starting coordinates, integer code could rasterize the span while the FPU calculated the reciprocal needed for the next correction. Abrash details this pipelining explicitly. Source

the FDIV to calculate the reciprocal of 1/z is overlapped with drawing 16 pixels, taking advantage of the Pentium's ability to perform floating-point in parallel with integer instructions, so the FDIV effectively takes only one cycle. — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 70, "Rasterization".

"Effectively" is key. The divide still required many cycles to finish; useful integer work concealed the waiting time. This relied on having work ready that did not depend on its result. Short runs, setup, and cleanup did not become free. In the sixteen-pixel assembly drawer Abrash is describing, the comment next to FDIV identifies the overlap as the purpose of the preceding setup (WinQuake/d_draw16.s:234-236); the eight-pixel drawer bears the same comment (WinQuake/d_draw.s:230-232). Code

The source also settles which drawer that account describes. On x86, d_subdiv16 defaults to 1 and selects the sixteen-pixel drawer; clearing it selects the eight-pixel one, and the portable build has only the eight-pixel path (WinQuake/d_init.c:27, 148-155). Code Abrash's sixteen-pixel account is the default x86 configuration, not every build or setting.

#Precision was part of the budget

Drawing pixels during a divide hid much of the wait. Lower precision made the divide itself shorter. In chapter 63's "Projection", Abrash reports 19 cycles for single-precision division; earlier in the chapter he gives 39 at full precision and 33 at double precision. These are Pentium instruction timings, not frame-rate measurements.

The cost was altered arithmetic. Abrash cautions that reducing FPU precision also affects other operations and can alter clipping results or the tolerances needed for comparisons. Quake's rendering code makes the trade explicit: it calls Sys_LowFPPrecision with a comment stating the purpose is to make FDIV fast, and that leaving this mode active globally would reduce timing precision after extended execution (WinQuake/r_main.c:972-976). It restores high precision before exiting that rendering routine (WinQuake/r_main.c:1047-1048). Code

Quake used lower precision while rendering, then restored it for the rest of the engine. The runtime walkthrough tracks the control word changes.

#A target, not a portability ban

Chapter 68 cites 7.5 cycles per mapped pixel for the tuned Pentium loop. That's for a loop given spans and surfaces to sample. Visibility, surfaces, entities, audio, and gameplay still consumed time. Instruction pairing and divide hiding don't imply a 2x overall speedup.

Portable versions persisted alongside the optimized code. Carmack's 23 November 1996 worklog clarifies the distinction, discussing an ordered Alpha NT machine:

.plan. We aren't going to be able to spend time writing assembly language for it like the intel version has, but it should still run at a pretty decent clip. — Carmack, 23 November 1996.

That records an intended port and a limit on available effort, not measured Alpha performance. It also bounds the chapter's claim: the renderer was portable, while equal tuning across processors was not promised. The Pentium influenced the division of work, the arithmetic precision, and the schedule of the inner loop. Those choices made sense together on the target machine; carrying the C elsewhere did not carry the same costs with it.

05 · ShippingShipping constraints: bandwidth, memory, deadline

Quake's fast renderer had to coexist with the realities of 1996: it needed to run well on modest machines, work over unreliable dial-up connections, and ship on a tight timeline. The traces of how id Software navigated those constraints are scattered throughout the code and developer accounts. Carmack's retroactive realization about modem latencies, logs tracking memory optimizations, and a last-minute collision workaround all tell parts of this story. This chapter examines the key technical tradeoffs id made to ship Quake under these limitations, setting aside the rendering decisions covered earlier. The June 1996 .plan updates give a sense of the final sprint; later developer commentaries fill in some of the rationale, without mythologizing every cut feature as a deadline sacrifice.

First pass. Quake's original networking targeted fast connections, its memory footprint shrank to fit small machines at the cost of sound quality, and rough edges remained for the sake of shipping. Recovery logic worked around physics failures, reduced precision freed up heap, and interpolation disguised latency. Next: QuakeC, which made the game rules replaceable without rebuilding the engine.

#The connection the design expected

An illuminating admission comes from Carmack's worklog on 2 August 1996, a month after launch, as he was developing QuakeWorld. He directly states the latency expectation he had designed around and how reality differed. His numbers describe the connections under discussion, not a comprehensive study of the playerbase. Source

My original design was targeted at <200ms connection latencies. […] Ok, I made a bad call. I have a T1 to my house, so I just wasn't familliar with PPP life. I'm adressing it now. — John Carmack, 2 August 1996.

Bandwidth and latency were separate but linked constraints. Sending updates more often could reduce input-to-response delays. But sending them faster than dial-up links could handle would congest the connection, delaying everything queued behind them. Carmack describes Quake's release netcode as simulating at 20 fps by default. He cautions that raising this could easily saturate modem bandwidth and cause punishing delays.

This reveals a tradeoff, but doesn't prove 20 Hz was empirically optimal for 1996 connections. Client and server frame rates could also vary. The key historical point is that past some threshold, shorter server frames could degrade responsiveness as the network became the bottleneck. The runtime trace of client-server messaging elaborates the flow involved.

#Replace stale state instead of waiting for it

Quake's netcode changed substantially prior to release. Carmack's August notes describe the initial reliable-ordered foundation and the unreliable channel grafted onto it later. Abrash's QuakeWorld summary in the Graphics Programming Black Book explains what necessitated this retrofit: waiting to retransmit dropped packets produced unplayable hitching. Time-critical game state moved to unreliable delivery. Reliable transport remained for data that couldn't simply be overwritten by updates.

This worked because updates only sent relevant state. Chapter 2 covered how PVS became a network optimization as well as a rendering one. The delta encoder (WinQuake/sv_main.c:472-512) further compresses by comparing fields to fixed baselines, not just the last update. Code The discussion of baseline deltas details the format. A key property emerges: unlike chains of incremental deltas, these snapshots stay decodable when one is lost. There are still no guarantees that every entity update fits in a single packet.

Unreliable delivery didn't eliminate latency entirely. Abrash describes how Quake hid jitter by keeping the visible world a bit behind the latest server time and interpolating. Carmack's 10 August entry confirms that release Quake buffered to smooth inconsistent packet arrival. The interpolation code itself shows the mechanism. At launch, fluidity and responsiveness remained in tension. QuakeWorld's advancements are best left for chapter 8.

#Memory changed what the player received

Abrash's Lighting Model chapter identifies 8 MB PCs as Quake's baseline when discussing texture caching. Chapter 3 explored how mipmaps and PVS made that cache viable. But memory pressures reached further.

On May 1st, Carmack logged an automatic sound quality fallback based on available memory:

.plan. * automatic sound decimation based on available memory — Carmack, 1 May 1996.

The final sound setup code cuts samples to 8-bit when the heap given to Quake is under 8 MB (WinQuake/snd_dma.c:196-200). Code It's based on the application heap size, not total system RAM. This reveals a concrete quality dial: sound precision could drop to save space. The worklog's "decimation" shouldn't be taken as a literal description of the final code's behavior though.

Four days later, another memory-sensitive feature:

.plan. * use all memory between the hunks as dynamic cache — Carmack, 5 May 1996.

This refers to Quake's general resource cache, separate from the surface cache. The overview of heap allocation explains how the cache resides in slack between the two hunk sentinels, reclaimable when either needs to grow. Assets can stick around while memory is plentiful without permanently reserving space.

Carmack also added an option to simulate a low-memory configuration. On May 30th he logged -minmemory, "for testing entry case." It caps Quake's heap on the command line, regardless of physical RAM (WinQuake/host.c:1035-1046). Code Its presence strengthens the evidence: not just a stated memory target, but a way to force even well-equipped developer machines to exercise it.

#The rewrite that did not happen before release

The networking transition changed a fundamental assumption without replacing the architecture built around it. Carmack calls the old code a reliable-stream design retrofitted for unreliable packets. Abrash's retrospective says debugging the resulting system continued until shipping. Source

John did not rewrite the net code to reflect this new fundamental assumption, and wound up with 8,000 lines of messy code that took right up until Quake shipped to debug. — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 70, "QuakeWorld".

The line count is Abrash's report, not a count reproduced here. More significantly, neither account establishes that a fixed deadline alone caused the decision against rewriting. They establish an architectural retrofit and a debugging cost that lasted through release. The June worklogs show that tail: 14 June records a fix for stuttering on dropped packets while still listing reliable-message backlog and packet-overflow checks as concerns.

The distinction matters when reading the code. A mechanism can survive because replacing it remains expensive even after its original assumption has changed. Here that interpretation is supported by both developers' later accounts, rather than inferred solely from complicated source.

On June 18th, Carmack describes a narrow bugfix:

.plan. * big hack to catch rare stuck occasions — Carmack, 18 June 1996.

SV_CheckStuck has a similar "big hack for rare failures" comment, attempting recovery via the previous position and perturbation before declaring the player stuck (WinQuake/sv_phys.c:754-760,768-799). 06 · QuakeCQuakeC: Why a Virtual Machine

In Quake, a new map changes where the player goes, but new game code changes what happens there - how monsters attack, what weapons fire, and which actions score points. By making the game rules reside in an interpreted progs.dat file, Quake allowed such changes without rebuilding the engine. id Software had to decide where to draw the boundary: mod authors could replace behaviors while the engine retained control over rendering, networking, and movement mechanics.

#From Editing Files to Changing Rules

id's 1993 DOOM announcement had promised an "open game" by documenting file formats for custom content. Quake took this further by making behavior executable. While the sources don't pinpoint exactly when id chose a virtual machine, QuakeC was in use before release:

It includes quake.exe winded.exe progs.dat, and a zip of the latest .qc files. — Carmack, .plan, 13 September 1996

Publishing replaceable game code, not just assets, let mod authors change the rules that give content meaning.

#Where the Engine Stops

The boundary is clear in entity callbacks. The engine handles movement and collision, then invokes QuakeC touch and think functions for game-specific responses. ED_LoadFromFile looks up an entity's classname to call its spawn function, so maps can introduce new object types.

Conversely, QuakeC uses engine builtins to spawn entities, trace lines, play sounds, and move objects, accessing engine capabilities through controlled entry points. A mod author has considerable flexibility without every subsystem being replaceable.

#A Program That Travels With the Game

progs.dat contains instructions, functions, strings, variables, and globals for Quake's interpreter, not native code. This allows engine ports to run the same game logic, although maps and assets must still be compatible.

The concrete interface has the loader check shared definitions and size entities based on the program's field count. Conventions like OP_STATE for monster animation are baked into the instruction set. It's a small, game-specific machine.

#Still Being Built Near Release

March 1996 entries show the machinery evolving alongside the game:

crunched progs.dat file structure culled unused edict fields — Carmack, 13 March 1996

* made an execution stack for progs — Carmack, 15 March 1996

Diagnosis features like per-function instruction counts and runaway loop checks aided authors without guarding against malicious code. Validation was still the engine's job.

#The Bill Came Due in Quake II

On 13 March 1997, Carmack proposed native game DLLs for Quake II, tying it to dropping DOS support:

I am strongly considering dropping qc in Quake 2 in favor of exporting most of the game logic to a seperate .dll file. This wasn't an option when we had to support dos, but I think it is the correct choice now. — Carmack, .plan, 13 March 1997

He forecast a 5-10% full-game speedup with less gain for simple mods, wanted better language and debugging features, but acknowledged the cost: per-platform compilation, security issues with untrusted native code, and a higher barrier to entry.

QuakeC had become an onramp to programming that DLLs would block for some tinkerers. Ambitious mods might run faster but be harder to share.

#What Survived the Language

By November 1998, Carmack returned to interpreted code for Quake Arena's client-side mods. Having players automatically receive native modules posed risks.

The only real solution to these problems is to use an interpreted language like Quake 1 did. I have reached the conclusion that the benefits of a standard language outweigh the benefits of a custom language for our purposes. — Carmack, .plan, 3 November 1998

This separated two bundled decisions: making game logic a portable program, and inventing QuakeC to express it. The VM's portability remained valuable even if the language could be swapped. Later games kept renegotiating the tradeoffs of that separation.

07 · VQuakeVQuake: the surface cache on hardware

When id Software ported Quake to Rendition's Vérité graphics card, they didn't completely abandon the existing renderer. Instead, VQuake maintained the CPU's role in edge sorting and surface building, then passed the completed spans to the Vérité chip for final rendering. This division of labor enabled a smoother, faster gaming experience, but it also introduced potential bottlenecks, such as bus transfers and the limitations of the card's video memory. VQuake demonstrates how much of a software architecture can persist through a hardware transition — and how the work it retains can ultimately constrain performance.

First pass. The Vérité's programmability allowed it to handle Quake's spans, enabling VQuake to preserve the software renderer's visibility calculations and cached lighting. This approach saved pixel work on a chip with restricted fill rate, but at the expense of CPU cycles, data transfers, and cache pressure. As hardware capabilities evolved, this balance would shift, and maintaining a separate renderer would incur additional costs. In the next chapter, we'll explore the decisions revisited in GLQuake, QuakeWorld, and after the game's release.

#A graphics card that could follow Quake's plan

In chapter 70 of his Graphics Programming Black Book, "Verite Quake," Michael Abrash identifies VQuake as the first hardware-accelerated Quake. Fabien Sanglard's 2019 history of the V1000 pinpoints its release to December 2, 1996, crediting Rendition's Walt Donovan and Stefan Podell, in collaboration with id's Michael Abrash. These specifics come from Sanglard's later account. Approximately seven weeks later, Carmack's January 22, 1997 worklog records the preliminary release of GLQuake.

According to Donovan's recollection in Sanglard's article, the V1000 combined a programmable 25 MHz RISC processor with graphics operations. It featured a bilinear-load instruction for reading and filtering 2×2 texel blocks but lacked dedicated hardware for depth testing, requiring the program to handle comparisons. This doesn't imply VQuake omitted a depth buffer; rather, depth testing consumed cycles on the programmable processor.

The Vérité's programmability provided an opportunity. Conventional triangle interfaces require the card to convert polygons into pixels. However, Quake already had code determining which horizontal runs of world pixels should be visible. Given the right programming, the Vérité could process these runs directly.

Abrash states that performance demands precluded using standard APIs for this port, which instead relied on Rendition's Speedy3D, augmented with custom calls and Vérité code. Source

The need to squeeze as much performance as possible out of Verite ruled out the use of a standard API such as Direct 3D or OpenGL — Michael Abrash, Graphics Programming Black Book, ch. 70, "Verite Quake".

This claim pertains to the specific needs of this chip and game at the time, not a general assertion about proprietary APIs being inherently faster or OpenGL being universally unsuitable for Rendition hardware.

#The polygon path that gave way to spans

A piece of the development timeline absent from the local archive is filled in by Stefan Podell's October 30, 1997 account, preserved on Vogons Wiki. The initial port utilized Quake's polygon interface, which yielded good filtering but disappointing performance. Donovan and Abrash then suggested leveraging the software renderer's span interface.

By having the CPU handle world visibility sorting, the card would have fewer pixels to draw and could render the world without depth comparisons. Depth values were still written, with comparisons enabled for objects like monsters. Custom microcode was developed to process Quake's span format. Podell succinctly describes the tradeoff: Source

we traded more CPU work in stage one for less Verite work in stage four — Stefan Podell, 30 October 1997, preserved on Vogons Wiki.

Abrash emphasizes limited fill rate at higher resolutions, while Podell highlights the V1000's subpar performance on depth-tested pixels. These accounts shed light on different aspects of the same decision. Sorting spans reduced both overdraw and the need for world depth comparisons. The software visibility walkthrough explains how Quake generates these spans.

#What crossed the bus

The CPU retained two significant responsibilities: constructing the edge and span lists representing visible world pixels, and combining texture tiles with lighting to create surfaces. The Vérité transferred span descriptors and finished surfaces into onboard memory, then texture-mapped the spans. Abrash explicitly describes this division: Source

VQuake uses an edge list and builds span lists on the CPU, just as in software Quake, then Verite DMAs the span descriptors to onboard memory and draws them. — Abrash, ch. 70, "Verite Quake".

For standard cached world surfaces, the work was partitioned as follows:

WorkSoftware QuakeVQuake
Determine visible spansCPUCPU
Combine texture and lightingCPUCPU
Keep the completed surfaceSystem-memory cacheOnboard cache, after transfer
Sample the surface and draw spansCPUVérité

Direct Memory Access (DMA) allows a device to transfer data without burdening the CPU with copying every byte. However, it still consumes bus bandwidth and competes for memory access. A surface already in the card's cache avoids another build and download; a missing or invalidated surface must be provided before it can be drawn. Chapter 3 explores the caching decision, and the runtime guide covers the software implementation.

The edge list benefited the cache as well as the pixel count. A world surface in the potentially visible set might be completely hidden from the current viewpoint. By sorting spans, such surfaces could be eliminated before the CPU built a lit surface and sent it to the card. VQuake carried this benefit from the software renderer across the bus boundary.

#A cache miss became more expensive

Software Quake's cached surfaces stored 8-bit palette indices, while VQuake required 16-bit RGB surfaces for hardware filtering, doubling the bytes per texel. Abrash reports that building these surfaces took about twice as long, not including the download cost. In his chapter 69 discussion, he estimates a cache miss in VQuake to be well over twice as expensive as in software Quake. Source

it takes about twice as long to build 16-bpp surfaces as 8-bpp surfaces, so the cost of missing the surface cache is well over twice as expensive in VQuake as in Quake. — Abrash, ch. 69, "Surface Caching with Hardware Assistance".

The card's 4 MB was shared among cached surfaces, display pages, and the depth buffer. Increasing resolution squeezed this budget from both ends: larger display buffers left less space, while more detailed surface mip levels demanded more of it. A cache sufficient for one view could struggle when a turn revealed a different set of walls.

This explains why a good average frame rate didn't guarantee a consistent response. Abrash identifies turning corners into complex scenes and spinning as situations where VQuake could feel more sluggish than software Quake. Carmack had already noticed the rebuild cost in August, before the public release. Source

.plan. We do take a bit of a hit when we have to generate a lot of 16 bit surfaces, so occasionally the framerate gets a bit uneven, but overall it is a very solid improvement. — Carmack, 22 August 1996.

The same entry mentions better image quality and nearly double the software speed at 512×384, without specifying the test machine or benchmark. These figures should be considered his report, not a universal performance guarantee. Both accounts highlight the key relationship: faster drawing made the cost of preparing new surfaces harder to conceal.

#Why not draw the light separately?

The alternative was well understood: render the tiled texture, then overlay the lightmap with blending. This approach avoids storing and transferring separately lit versions of each surface. Lighting remains aligned with the texture under perspective without relying on a composite-cache hit.

However, the saved memory work becomes additional pixel work. Abrash states that two-pass lighting required at least twice the fill rate, which the Vérité couldn't afford at higher resolutions. Source

However, this approach requires at least twice the fill rate of the surface cache approach, and Verite didn't have enough fill rate for that at higher resolutions. — Abrash, ch. 70, "Verite Quake".

GLQuake pursued this path on hardware better suited to it. Abrash's adjacent "GLQuake" section reports good performance on fast 3dfx chips and poor performance on many contemporary accelerators. Carmack's December hardware survey provides context for the difference: he reported approximately 50 million textured pixels per second for 3dfx, with minimal sensitivity to enabled options. This fill-rate observation, not a Quake frame rate, explains why extra passes could be feasible there.

VQuake also preserved a possibility Abrash still valued: the CPU could composite procedural effects into surfaces before the card drew them. He proposed bumps, shimmers, and warps as potential future applications. These were architectural possibilities, not a list of shipped effects. Surface caching remained a justifiable choice, even as another hardware design made two-pass lighting more appealing.

#Faster silicon did not remove the CPU work

The same division that helped the V1000 could limit an upgrade. In his October 1997 account, Podell explains that VQuake still spent substantial CPU time sorting spans and building textures on the faster V2x00 chips. A quicker pixel renderer couldn't eliminate those stages.

This is the long-term consequence of the port's success. VQuake reduced the work sent to the graphics processor by doing more on the Pentium. Once drawing became cheaper, the remaining CPU work constituted a larger share of the frame. Taking advantage of a new chip could necessitate revisiting that division, rather than simply running the old renderer on it. Inference

#The cost of keeping another renderer

By February 23, 1997, Carmack wanted a rendering architecture that could combine software Quake, GLQuake, and a Windows VQuake in one executable. On April 2, he still described the Vérité module as work to come. These are plans; neither entry records a completed module or an abandonment date.

The maintenance problem becomes explicit in May, when Carmack explains why he would not add a native Glide renderer despite expecting a possible 10–15% gain over the OpenGL driver. He already had software and OpenGL paths to maintain and had committed to Vérité support. Source

.plan. The big drawback is that every codepath that gets added holds back future innovation. Just having software and gl is a lot of work, and I have allready commited to verite support. — Carmack, 12 May 1997.

This explains the pressure against adding vendor-specific paths; it is not an announcement that VQuake was cancelled. It also answers a different question from the fill-rate comparison. Pixel throughput determined which lighting technique suited a card. Engineering time determined how many distinct implementations id could keep improving.

VQuake had shown that Quake's surface cache could survive hardware acceleration. It had also shown the price: custom card code, a carefully chosen division of CPU and accelerator work, and another renderer to carry forward. The next chapter follows what happened when id chose to revisit those commitments.
