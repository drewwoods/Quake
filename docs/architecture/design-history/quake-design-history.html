Companion to Life of a Frame. That guide answers what the code does, in the order a frame does it. This one answers why it exists and how it got that way, in the order the decisions were made. Neither repeats the other: where the mechanism matters, this guide links across rather than re-explaining it; a dashed link with an arrow opens the other guide.

Reader's guide

Quake's source reads like a set of answers with the questions filed off. The expanded collision hulls, the precomputed visibility, the surface cache, the 20 Hz server tick — each is a settled solution to a problem that was live and unsolved in 1995, and several are the survivors of approaches that were built, measured, and thrown away. This guide reconstructs those questions from the public record: Michael Abrash's contemporaneous chapters, the developers' .plan files, and id's own announcements, all archived under docs/historical/.

The material here is evidence-bounded. A claim about what the code does is checkable against the code and is marked with a source reference. A claim about what someone was thinking is only as good as the document it came from, so those are quoted and attributed rather than paraphrased into confident narrative. Where the record is thin, the guide says so instead of filling the gap.

Dates are as published. .plan files were daily worklogs written for a public finger query, not design documents — they are terse, occasionally wrong, and record what was done that day rather than why. Abrash's chapters were written during and shortly after the work, and are the closest thing to a design record that exists.

01 · OriginsBefore Quake: What DOOM Could Not Do

Quake's architecture is easier to read as a list of answers to DOOM's limits than as a design in its own right. DOOM shipped in December 1993 with a renderer built on a two-dimensional binary space partition, sprites for every moving thing, and a fixed camera orientation. Each of those was a deliberate trade that bought a playable frame rate on a 386, and each became a wall that the next engine had to go through rather than around. This chapter establishes what that wall was, using id's own 1993 announcement, Carmack's later retrospective on the DOOM code, and Romero's 1994 announcement of Quake — which described the game before its engine existed.

First pass. DOOM's BSP tree was two-dimensional and was used only for drawing. Quake's is three-dimensional and is used for drawing, collision, and network visibility alike. Almost everything distinctive about Quake's runtime follows from widening that one structure and then finding work for it. Next: the year of prototypes that decided how to use it.

#The 1993 feature list, read as a ceiling

id's DOOM press release of 1 January 1993 is unusually specific about what the engine would do (doom_press_release_1993.txt). It advertises a "fantastic fully texture-mapped environment," non-orthogonal walls, light diminishing and light sourcing, variable-height floors and ceilings, environment animation, palette translation, and four-player networked play. Carmack is quoted with a performance target: "on a 486/33, we're talking 35 frames per second, fully texture-mapped at normal detail, for a large area of the screen."

The list is worth reading twice, because what it omits is the actual subject of this guide. There is no mention of looking up or down, of rooms stacked over rooms, of geometry at an angle to the floor, or of three-dimensional creatures. Those are not oversights in the marketing copy; they are outside what the engine could represent. DOOM's world is a floor plan with heights attached. Walls are vertical, floors and ceilings are horizontal, and no two sectors can occupy the same footprint at different heights.

Two features on that list, however, survive into Quake essentially unchanged, and it is worth naming them now because later chapters depend on them. The first is light diminishing and light sourcing — the observation that lighting is what makes an environment feel like a place, and therefore that lighting is worth spending the frame budget on. Quake's answer is far more ambitious and is covered in the planned chapter on surface caching, but the commitment is already visible here. The second is the section headed "An Open Game":

When our last hit, WOLFENSTEIN 3D was released the public responded with an almost immediate deluge of home-brewed utilities; map editors, sound editors, trainers, etc. All without any help on file formats or game layout from Id Software. DOOM will be release as an OPEN GAME. We will provide file formats and technical notes for anyone who wants them.

That is a 1993 statement of an architectural policy that Quake pushes much further, from published file formats to a shipped bytecode virtual machine with the game logic compiled into a replaceable progs.dat. The QuakeC VM is the direct descendant of this paragraph; the planned QuakeC chapter will trace that line.

#A binary tree in two dimensions

DOOM's central data structure was a BSP tree, and Carmack's use of it is the reason the technique became widely known. Michael Abrash, writing in 1995 while working alongside him, is explicit about the dimensionality: while searching for Quake's visibility algorithm, Carmack "spent a week on this, as a byproduct devising a perfect DOOM (2-D) visibility architecture, whereby a single, linear walk of a DOOM BSP tree produces zero-overdraw 2-D visibility" (Black Book, ch. 64, "Quake's Visible-Surface Determination"). The parenthesis is doing real work. A DOOM BSP splits the map with lines on a plan view; every splitter is a wall segment, and the tree partitions a floor plan.

That constrains the geometry directly. Because the splitters are vertical, the tree divides a two-dimensional floor plan; vertical walls are represented by the map's linedefs, while horizontal floors and ceilings remain sector properties that the renderer fills in between walls. Carmack described exactly this structure as the thing he would change, in the notes accompanying the DOOM source release on 23 December 1997 (carmack_doom_retrospective.txt):

The way the rendering proceded from walls to floors to sprites could be collapsed into a single front-to-back walk of the bsp tree to collect information, then draw all the contents of a subsector on the way back up the tree. It requires treating floors and ceilings as polygons, rather than just the gaps between walls, and it requires clipping sprite billboards into subsector fragments, but it would be The Right Thing.

Quake removes that constraint at the root of the representation. Its splitting planes carry a full normal vector, not an implied vertical:

The key difference is that normal is a vec3_t. Polygons can sit at any angle, so ramps and arbitrary architecture become expressible; leaves are convex volumes rather than convex floor regions, so rooms can stack; and the tree can answer questions about points anywhere in space rather than points on a plan. The cost is a much harder compile. Abrash's chapter 60, "Compiling BSP Trees" builds one in two dimensions, over line segments, to teach the technique; doing it over arbitrary 3-D polygons is qbsp's job, and the splitter heuristic that made it tractable is recorded in chapter 64. The harder consequence is visibility, which the year of discarded renderers covers. The on-disk BSP format and the node and leaf graph it loads into are documented in Life of a Frame.

#The BSP that only drew

The dimensional change is the obvious difference. The more consequential one is what the tree gets used for, and here the best source is Carmack assessing his own code four years later:

The movement and line of sight checking against the lines is one of the bigger misses that I look back on. It is messy code that had some failure cases, and there was a vastly simpler (and faster) solution sitting in front of my face. I used the BSP tree for rendering things, but I didn't realize at the time that it could also be used for environment testing.

In DOOM, the BSP tree served the renderer, and gameplay queries — did this shot hit, can this monster see the player, where does this movement stop — ran against a separate representation, the raw line list, with its own bugs. Quake derives both kinds of query from the same compiled map geometry. Rendering walks the render BSP; collision walks one of the pre-expanded clip hulls, reusing the map's planes. Life of a Frame covers the mechanism in detail: SV_RecursiveHullCheck is the descent, and contents types explain how the engine keeps render nodes and collision hulls separate.

Carmack's retrospective continues straight into the price of doing it that way:

Sweeping volumes for movement gets a bit tougher, and touches on many of the challenges faced in quake / quake2 with edge bevels on polyhedrons.

This is the origin of one of Quake's most distinctive and least intuitive structures. Sweeping a player-sized box through a 3-D tree is genuinely hard; reducing that box to a point moving through a tree whose solid volumes have been pre-expanded by the box's dimensions is easy, and reuses the descent unchanged. So qbsp compiles not one tree but several, and each model carries all of them:

with MAX_MAP_HULLS fixed at 4 in WinQuake/bspfile.h:24. The world model uses hulls 0, 1, and 2; slot 3 is unused by the original runtime. SV_HullForEntity picks between them purely on width (WinQuake/world.c:147-153): under 3 units gets the point hull, up to 32 gets hull 1, anything larger gets hull 2. Players and most monsters therefore share hull 1, 32x32x56; hull 2, 64x64x88, is for the large monsters (WinQuake/model.c:959-981).

That is why body sizes come in discrete classes rather than being per-entity: the dimensions are compiled into the map, not chosen at runtime. It is not the reason crouching is absent. Hull 1's vertical extent is standing height, and shrinking an entity's bounding box does not change the hull that is actually traced against, so a crouch would need its own size class — and slot 3, which the format reserves, was never filled. The archive does not record why. The three hulls and the way an entity selects or fabricates one are covered in Life of a Frame. The "edge bevels" Carmack mentions are the correction needed to keep expanded polyhedra convex — a detail that only becomes a problem once you have committed to this approach.

The third use of the tree has no direct DOOM counterpart. Because a leaf is a volume, and because visibility between volumes can be computed offline, the same spatial structure that answers "what do I draw" can answer "what does this client need to be told about." Quake's server gates entity updates on the potentially visible set of the leaf each client occupies. That is a networking decision built on a rendering data structure, and the planned shipping-constraints chapter will examine how dialup bandwidth shaped it.

#Announced as a design, not an engine

Quake was public long before it was real. John Romero's .plan of 18 March 1994 announces it by name, with a subtitle, a comparison, and a start date (john_romero/1994-03-18.txt):

.plan. The next game is going to blow DOOM all to hell. DOOM totally sucks in comparison to our next game, QUAKE: The Fight for Justice! QUAKE is going to be a bigger step over DOOM than DOOM was over Wolf3D (ya know -- DOOM = pong). We won't start developing QUAKE until Fall'94, due to Jaguar DOOM (Comlynxed!), commercial DOOM (COMPUSA, Babbage's, etc.) and maybe a DOOM level pack.

Two things are worth extracting. The first is the ambition, stated as a ratio rather than a feature: the step from DOOM to Quake was to be at least as large as the step from Wolfenstein to DOOM. Wolfenstein's walls were axis-aligned and eight feet thick; DOOM's could be at any angle and any thickness. The equivalent leap was a full third dimension, and the planned chapters on discarded renderers, surface caching, and one-processor design describe the cost of having promised it.

The second is the schedule. Quake is announced in March 1994, development is not to begin until autumn, and the game shipped in June 1996. Abrash did not join id until March 1995 (Black Book, ch. 64), by which point "John already had an engine prototyped and a plan in mind" — a plan that the year of discarded renderers examines, and that almost entirely failed to survive contact with the problem. The title The Fight for Justice did not survive either. What was shipped shares an ambition with the announcement and very little else, which is a useful calibration on how much of Quake's design was intended in advance versus discovered under deadline.

The same .plan records the development environment in passing — "To get the entire world running NEXTSTEP for development" — and NeXTSTEP is why the source contains a NeXT build path and why the tools were written the way they were.

#What Quake had to close

Reading the two announcements against each other gives a concrete list of what changed, and the roadmap above organizes the rest of this guide around the expensive items on it. The chapter numbers in the last column refer to planned chapters.

The polygon counts are Abrash's: "a good-sized Quake level contains on the order of 10,000 polygons, about three times as many polygons as a comparable DOOM level" (ch. 64). The DOOM timebase is Carmack's own aside in the source release notes, where he lists "there are some issues with DOOM's 35 hz timebase" among the obstacles to a hardware-accelerated port. Quake lets the host use a measured delta while a dedicated server targets sys_ticrate; a listen server still runs one server tick inside each accepted host frame. Remote clients therefore need to interpolate entity positions between received states.

One caveat on the evidence in this chapter. The retrospective quoted above was written in December 1997 — after Quake and Quake II. It is reliable testimony about how Carmack understood DOOM's limits in hindsight, and it lines up well with what Quake actually does, but it is not evidence of what he intended in 1994. The contemporaneous record of that period is thin: the March 1994 .plan is the announcement, and there is no public design document between it and Abrash's arrival a year later. Where this guide describes intent before March 1995, it is inference from the shipped code, and says so.

02 · VisibilityThe Year of Discarded Renderers

Between March 1995 and the middle of 1996, Quake's visible-surface determination was rebuilt from scratch repeatedly. A beam tree was written, tuned, and abandoned. At least five other approaches were prototyped or seriously argued and dropped. The design that shipped — precompute, for every leaf in the map, the set of leaves potentially visible from it, then at runtime draw only those — was not on the original plan and arrived within roughly a year of Abrash's joining, well before release. This chapter follows that sequence, because the shape of the surviving code is unintelligible without it: the potentially visible set looks like an obvious idea in hindsight, and reading it as an obvious idea gets its cost model exactly backwards.

First pass. Quake's problem was never "how do I draw a polygon fast." It was overdraw: in a ten-thousand-polygon level most of what is inside the view frustum is hidden behind something else, and drawing it anyway is wasted work that scales with level complexity. The discarded approaches tried to make world visibility tractable at runtime. The one that shipped moved that work offline; runtime rendering still resolved pixel visibility. Next: what that decision made affordable.

#The problem, stated precisely

Abrash defines the target in chapter 64, "Quake's Visible-Surface Determination", and the definition is worth keeping in mind because every rejected approach failed against one clause of it:

The process of VSD would ideally work as follows: First, you would cull all polygons that are completely outside the view frustum (view pyramid), and would clip away the irrelevant portions of any polygons that are partially outside. Then, you would draw only those pixels of each polygon that are actually visible from the current viewpoint [...] Finally, in a perfect world, the tests to figure out what parts of which polygons are visible would be free, and the processing time would be the same for all possible viewpoints, giving the game a smooth visual flow.

Two parts were tractable. A BSP makes coarse frustum culling cheap, because each node bounds a convex subspace, so rejecting one node rejects every polygon beneath it. It also supplies a front-to-back or back-to-front traversal. The hard part was the middle clause and, critically, the last one.

The last clause is the one that does the damage. A painter's-algorithm renderer draws every polygon in the frustum, hidden or not; Abrash estimates worst-case overdraw in a Quake level "10 times or more," meaning each pixel written ten times on average. The problem is not only that this is slow. It is that best case and worst case diverge, so the frame rate swings as the player turns. A design that is fast in a corridor and unplayable in an atrium has not solved the problem.

#The prototype, and how long it lasted

Abrash joined id in March 1995, expecting to optimize an existing design:

When I arrived at id at the beginning of March 1995, John already had an engine prototyped and a plan in mind, and I assumed that our work was a simple matter of finishing and optimizing that engine. If I had been aware of id's history, however, I would have known better.

The correction is the useful part. By Abrash's count Carmack had by then written "perhaps 20 distinct engines over a four-year period," including "four engines in four weeks" on one occasion. Rewriting the renderer was the normal state of the project, not a crisis in it. Three months after Abrash arrived, "only one element of the original VSD design was anywhere in sight."

This is the single most important piece of context for reading Quake's source. The code is not a first draft that was optimized; it is the last survivor of a long elimination.

#The beam tree

The original design drew front-to-back and tracked, in a second BSP tree, which parts of the screen were still unpainted. That tree was three-dimensional: a beam tree, a set of wedges bounded by planes radiating from the viewpoint.

It starts as one beam — the frustum — with everything outside marked solid and the inside empty. Walking the world tree front-to-back, each polygon is converted into a beam by running planes from its edges through the viewpoint. Whatever part of that beam intersects empty space in the beam tree is visible, and is added to the beam tree as solid. When the beam tree is entirely solid, nothing further can be visible and the walk stops. Drawing happens afterwards: once the tree is complete, the visible portions of the polygons that contributed to it are rasterized.

The reason to use 3-D beams rather than a 2-D screen region is arithmetic. Every beam plane passes through the viewpoint, so testing a vertex against one is a dot product with the plane normal and a sign check, and generating a beam from a polygon edge is a single cross product. There is no projection and no division in the inner test.

It failed on the last clause of the specification. The early-out looks like it caps the worst case, but a view across the top of a level, or out to the sky, never fills the beam tree, so every polygon in the frustum gets tested. Numerical precision left thin cracks that also prevented the tree closing. Abrash's verdict:

in scenes with long view distances, such as views across the top of a level, the total cost of beam processing slowed Quake's frame rate to a crawl. So, in the end, the beam-tree approach proved to suffer from much the same malady as the painter's algorithm: The worst case was much worse than the average case, and it didn't scale well with increasing level complexity.

#Five that did not ship

Abrash lists the alternatives that were prototyped "in overnight or weekend-long bursts of coding" or argued and dropped. They are worth recording individually, because each one fails differently and the failures map the design space.

Subdividing raycast. Cast rays on an 8×8 screen grid — cheap, because a ray is just a clip into the BSP tree until it reaches a solid leaf. Where adjacent rays hit different surfaces, cast another between them, recursively, until neighbours agree or are adjacent pixels. This scales with pixel count rather than polygon count, and has no overdraw at all: exactly the right scaling behaviour. It was rejected for correctness, not speed — small polygons can fall between rays and disappear.

Vertex-free surfaces. Store the world as planes only, and extract polygons from plane intersections just before drawing. Clipping becomes fast and the dataset shrinks considerably, since a plane is far more compact than a polygon. Rejected because extracting polygons from planes is expensive at exactly the wrong moment. The shipped on-disk BSP does pool and share planes, but that is ordinary BSP economy rather than a survival of this idea: the vertex-free proposal was that polygons should not be stored at all.

The draw-buffer. An occlusion bitmap with one bit per pixel, recording only whether that pixel has been written. This eliminates overdraw, at the cost of a test in the inner loop, extra writes, cache misses, and, in Abrash's summary, "considerable complexity." The variations considered are a good illustration of how far the search went: test the buffer a byte at a time and skip fully-occluded bytes, or branch from each byte into one of 256 unrolled loops for drawing 0–8 pixels, with the hope of overlapping the perspective divide with pixel processing.

Span-based drawing. Rasterize polygons into spans, clip each new span against a global span list, keep only the nearest at each pixel. Front-to-back walking means little sorting is needed — if there is overlap, the span already in the list is nearer. No overdraw, but every polygon still has to be turned into spans, and the span arithmetic is substantial. This is the closest of the five to what shipped. The edge list and span sorting in the final renderer belong to the same family, though Abrash arrives at edge sorting separately in chapters 66 and 67 rather than by reviving this proposal. The section below returns to it.

Portals. Track the holes in surfaces, since line-of-sight only extends through them. Draw front-to-back; on reaching a portal, clip the polygons and portals behind it to its limits, recursively, until nothing visible remains. This draws only the visible parts of visible polygons — the ideal — but pays for it in a great deal of portal clipping.

The portal approach is the one to keep in view: the shipped PVS preserves its connectivity insight, but precomputes visibility per leaf instead of clipping portals at runtime.

#The breakthrough

Carmack next tried extracting visibility directly from the world BSP tree, reasoning that the beam tree was a second-order structure holding information the world tree already contained implicitly. A week of that produced, as a side effect, a perfect zero-overdraw visibility algorithm for two-dimensional DOOM trees — and, in three dimensions, mounting complexity and persistent glitches. Abrash's account of the weekend that followed is the most-quoted passage in the chapter:

When I came in on Monday, John had the look of a man who had broken through to the other side—and also the look of a man who hadn't had much sleep. [...] At 3:30 Monday morning, as he lay in bed, thinking about portals, he thought of precalculating and storing in each leaf a list of all leaves visible from that leaf, and then at runtime just drawing the visible leaves back-to-front for whatever leaf the viewpoint happens to be in, ignoring all other leaves entirely.

The move was to stop computing world visibility at runtime and start looking it up. The discarded approaches tried to make that calculation cheaper; the PVS removes it from the frame loop. Abrash draws the general lesson through a colleague's aphorism — "almost all programming can be viewed as an exercise in caching" — and calls the reframing the inspired part, rather than the algorithm.

The archive does not date this. What it bounds: Abrash joined in March 1995; chapter 64 was written "several months" into his time there, with "a few more months to go," describes the PVS prototype as already working, and was published while Quake was still unshipped; the game shipped in June 1996. That places the breakthrough in 1995, most likely around the middle of the year and so roughly a year before release. The archive supports no tighter figure than that.

One correction the chapter itself supplies, and which this guide should not sand off: Carmack did not experience it as a eureka. Abrash reports him saying "precalculating the PVS was a logical evolution of the approaches he had been considering, that there was no moment when he said 'Eureka!'" The dramatic version is Abrash's framing of a colleague's work; the participant's version is that it was a logical next step. Note also what the preceding week was actually spent on: extracting visibility directly from the world BSP tree, not portals. Portals enter at 3:30 on the Monday morning, as the thing he was thinking about when the precomputation idea arrived. Abrash reports both accounts within a few paragraphs of each other, and the gap between them is itself informative about how such designs arrive.

Nor was the underlying idea unprecedented. Abrash's own reference list for the chapter cites Seth Teller's Visibility Preprocessing for Interactive Walkthroughs (SIGGRAPH 91) and his dissertation on visibility in densely occluded polyhedral environments. The archive does not record whether that work was an input; it does establish that precomputed visibility was known in the literature, and that the achievement was making it fit in a 1996 consumer product.

#Making it fit

Abrash reports that the first raw, uncompressed PVS was several megabytes. Four changes brought it down to roughly 20 KB for a good-sized level:

  1. Store the set as a bit vector, one bit per leaf, rather than a list.
  2. Compress it with run-length encoding of zero bytes, which works extremely well because the rows are sparse. Life of a Frame covers the decompressor.
  3. Change the BSP splitter heuristic to produce fewer leaves. Abrash: "choosing as the next splitter the polygon that splits the fewest other polygons appears to be the best heuristic."
  4. Seal the outside of levels, so the compiler can discard exterior surfaces that can never be seen.

The fourth explains a rule every Quake mapper has met: a leak prevents the normal sealed-world BSP/PVS build from producing valid visibility data. That constraint is not arbitrary strictness — it is what makes the surface count, and therefore the leaf count, and therefore the size of the visibility data, tractable. The format allows up to a megabyte for it (MAX_MAP_VISIBILITY at WinQuake/bspfile.h:44), while the cited good-sized level used about a fiftieth of that.

#What it bought, and what it cost

The PVS is deliberately imprecise. It records what is visible from anywhere in a leaf, not from the actual viewpoint, so it over-reports. Abrash gives the resulting overdraw as "typically on the order of 50 percent but ranging up to 150 percent."

That inaccuracy is the entire point. Every rejected approach bought precision with runtime work that grew with scene complexity; the PVS accepts bounded imprecision in exchange for a lookup. The payoff is in the third clause of the original specification:

precalculating the PVS results in a leveling of performance; worst case is no longer much worse than best case, because there's no longer extra VSD processing—just more polygons and perhaps some extra overdraw—associated with complex scenes. The first time John showed me his working prototype, I went to the most complex scene I knew of, a place where the frame rate used to grind down into the single digits, and spun around smoothly, with no perceptible slowdown.

The cost moved to the map toolchain, where it is paid once by the author rather than sixty times a second by the player. That trade is why vis is slow enough to have its own folklore. The archive does not quantify vis itself, but it does show the stage becoming a prerequisite for the rest of the toolchain: Carmack's .plan of 27 August 1996, describing his new radiosity lighting tool, notes that "you MUST do a vis before running qrad (otherwise it would take about 1000 times longer)" — the thousandfold and the tool's 100 MB working set are qrad's, not vis's — and ends with him wanting "to look into" the algorithmic order of full vis processing, "especially now that the vis stage is put before production lighting." Life of a Frame covers how the runtime consumes the result; the GL path keeps a r_novis cvar (WinQuake/gl_rmain.c:86) that switches the whole mechanism off, which is the fastest way to feel what it does.

#The PVS stops being a renderer feature

The clearest evidence that this was a structural decision rather than a rendering optimization is what happened to it afterwards. Carmack's 1996 worklogs show the PVS being reached for by subsystems that have nothing to do with drawing. The entries are terse and follow a consistent shape, with * marking completed work and + marking immediate next steps; a plain line is a backlog item. Tracked across six weeks:

.plan. PVS for net update — 15 March 1996, backlog; then * changed network update to client PVS specific — 23 April 1996, done.

That is the moment visibility became a bandwidth policy: the server stopped telling each client about entities the client could not possibly see. The mechanism is SV_FatPVS, and the planned shipping-constraints chapter will examine how dialup shaped it. The same worklogs carry a one-line done-item, "fixed monster sighting PVS problem," on 11 March. That reads like gameplay-visible behaviour depending on compiled visibility data, but a single worklog line will not carry the claim on its own, and the archive says nothing further about it. On 6 April Carmack probes for further uses:

.plan. use PVS to speed line of sight checks? Is that any faster than doing the line check, because it involves two point checks? — Carmack, 6 April 1996

Note that this one is a question, and that it is asked on cost grounds rather than accepted on principle. It is a useful corrective to the tidy version of this story: by 1996 the PVS was infrastructure, but its applicability was still being evaluated case by case.

#What the PVS did not solve

The PVS answers "which leaves might be visible." It does not answer "which pixels are visible," and its deliberate over-reporting actively creates overdraw. Something still has to resolve the remainder.

That is where span-based drawing, rejected on its own above, returns. Abrash describes the shipped renderer at the end of the chapter as the PVS "together with a still-in-development sorted-edge rasterizer that completely eliminates overdraw." The two halves are complementary: the PVS discards most of the world for free, and the edge list resolves what is left exactly. Neither would have been sufficient alone, and the PVS's tolerance for imprecision is affordable precisely because a zero-overdraw rasterizer sits behind it.

Quake still uses a depth buffer for entities, particles and sprites. The world writes depth as it is rasterized, so dynamic geometry can be tested per pixel without making the world depend on a general z-buffer. That is a separate decision, reached in chapter 66, rather than the rejected draw-buffer returning: the draw-buffer was a one-bit occlusion mask for the world, and this is per-pixel depth for everything drawn after it. What they share is the recognition that an occlusion structure is affordable once it covers only the geometry that needs one.

The implementation details belong in Life of a Frame. The design decision is the cost split: the PVS discards most of the world cheaply, while the edge list and depth buffer resolve what remains at runtime.
